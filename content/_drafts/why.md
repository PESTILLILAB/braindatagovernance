Sharing brain data across borders used to be a technical problem. Today it is a legal one, and the rules change faster than any lab can follow.
{: .lead}

::cards
- **The science needs shared data** — No single lab can collect what it takes to understand the brain. The field spent twenty years making brain data portable: common formats, open archives, funders who expect data to be reused. Technically, the job is done.
- **The law is national; the data are not** — More than 150 countries now have data-protection laws. Most allow research, but each allowance stops at the border. A permission in one country does not travel with the data to the next.
- **Sovereignty is the new constraint** — Governments increasingly treat brain data as a strategic asset: keep it in the country, replace downloads with controlled access, screen research for national security. Collaborations slow down or never start.
- **Brain data give away more than they seem** — A brain scan can identify a face. Recordings of brain activity reveal attention, emotion and intention, and can be decoded into what a person saw or meant to say. The risk is not only who you are but what can be inferred about you.
- **AI keeps the data alive** — A model trained on brain data carries them forward in its weights. Delete the dataset and the model still knows. Governance that stops at the dataset stops too early.

## The facts on the ground

::cards
- **Almost no law mentions brain data** — They fall under health, biometric or sensitive data, categories written for other things. Whether a brain scan is "identifiable", and what counts as de-identified, differs from country to country.
- **Sharing is allowed everywhere and easy nowhere** — Across Africa, Europe and Latin America, nearly every cross-border pathway is permitted in principle and conditional in practice: extra safeguards, extra approvals, country-specific legal work. Only inside Europe is the path clear.
- **Contracts do the protecting** — Where the law is silent or uncertain, the agreement between institutions carries the obligations. That favours institutions with lawyers and leaves the rest to negotiate from scratch.
- **Oversight exists on paper** — Most countries have a data-protection authority and ethics review; far fewer have the capacity to supervise what happens to data after they leave.
- **The burden falls on researchers** — Every collaboration negotiates its own agreement from scratch, at every institution, for every dataset. Months of a scientist's time go into paperwork that is nearly identical each time, and goes slowest where legal support is scarcest.

## Why it is also about fairness

Brain data are under-collected in low- and middle-income regions, and sharing has mostly been a privilege of well-resourced institutions. Researchers there want to share; what they lack is legal support, clear national rules, training and trust. Governance built only for the well-resourced would make that gap wider. Ours is built with them: the BRIDGE workshops in Rio de Janeiro, Marrakesh and Johannesburg shaped the instruments, and the agreement includes modules for community governance, benefit sharing and capacity building.

## What changes with an open ecosystem

::cards
- **Protection travels with the data** — The obligations attached to a dataset are written down once, in a form people and machines can read, and follow it through every transfer, transformation and model.
- **Governance before the data move** — A collaboration can classify its data by what they can reveal, test the pathway against the law at both ends, and assemble the right agreement, before anything is sent.
- **Kept current by a community** — The map of the law, the tools and the agreements are maintained in the open by the people who use them, with legal reviewers in every region.
- **Measured, not asserted** — We will track what a cross-border agreement costs in time, and how many never get signed, before and after the instruments are used. If the burden does not fall, the instruments change.
